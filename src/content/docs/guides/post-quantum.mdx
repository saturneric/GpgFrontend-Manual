GpgFrontend lets you make and use **post-quantum keys** with a few clicks. You
do not need the command line. This page explains in plain words what that
means, why it might matter to you, and how to make your first one.

## Why You Might Need This

Today's encryption is safe because breaking it would take even the fastest normal
computer billions of years. The math is just too hard. Think of it as a lock that
no machine on Earth can pick.

**Quantum computers** are a new and very different kind of computer. A big enough
one could do that hard math quickly, and pick today's locks. Machines that powerful
don't exist yet. But there is a catch:

> Someone can copy your locked messages **today**, save them, and wait. When a
> strong quantum computer finally arrives, they unlock everything they saved, even
> messages from years ago.

This is called **"harvest now, decrypt later."** So the real question isn't "can
anyone read this today?" It's "does this need to stay secret for many years?"

If the answer is yes, it makes sense to start using new, **quantum-proof**
encryption now, before quantum computers catch up. This kind of encryption is
called **post-quantum**. NIST, the standards office of the United States, has
approved a new set of post-quantum algorithms. OpenPGP is starting to use them.

<Aside type="note" title="Changed in v2.2.3">

The rPGP engine now follows the finished standard for post-quantum OpenPGP
(RFC 9980). In v2.2.2 it followed an earlier draft of that standard.

</Aside>

<Aside type="tip" title="Do you actually need this?">

- **Probably not yet, for everyday use.** If your secrets only matter for a short
  while, today's normal keys are perfectly safe.
- **Worth a head start** if you protect things that must stay private for a decade
  or more, like medical, legal, or personal records.
- **Still experimental.** See the warning at the bottom of this page before you
  rely on these keys.

</Aside>

## What You Can Make

GpgFrontend offers the new NIST-approved post-quantum algorithms. Like normal
keys, they do one of two jobs. Some are for **encryption** (locking messages).
Some are for **signing** (proving a message is really from you).

A quick plain-words guide:

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="ML-KEM" icon="seti:lock">
    Once called Kyber. For **encryption**. GpgFrontend always pairs it with a
    classic algorithm. A pair like this is called a **hybrid**. Your
    protection is then never weaker than today's, even if one of the two is
    later found to have a flaw.
  </Card>
  <Card title="ML-DSA" icon="pencil">
    Once called Dilithium. For **signing**, also paired with a classic
    algorithm.
  </Card>
  <Card title="SLH-DSA" icon="pencil">
    Once called SPHINCS+. Also for **signing**. It is built on a very simple,
    well-trusted idea (hashing). This makes it the most cautious choice.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

All of them need the newer **version 6 (v6)** key format. GpgFrontend picks v6
for you when you choose a post-quantum option.

The table below lists the exact options for reference. The last column shows
the oldest engine version that can make each one.

| Algorithm              | OpenPGP Type | Purpose    | Engine support |
| ---------------------- | ------------ | ---------- | -------------- |
| ML-KEM-768 + X25519    | Hybrid KEM   | Encryption | <Badge text="GnuPG 2.5.0" variant="note" /> <Badge text="rPGP 0.1.2" variant="tip" /> |
| ML-KEM-1024 + X448     | Hybrid KEM   | Encryption | <Badge text="GnuPG 2.5.0" variant="note" /> <Badge text="rPGP 0.1.2" variant="tip" /> |
| ML-DSA-65 + Ed25519    | Hybrid sign  | Signing    | <Badge text="rPGP 0.1.2" variant="tip" /> |
| ML-DSA-87 + Ed448      | Hybrid sign  | Signing    | <Badge text="rPGP 0.1.2" variant="tip" /> |
| SLH-DSA-SHAKE-128S     | Hash-based   | Signing    | <Badge text="rPGP 0.1.2" variant="tip" /> |

## Make a Post-Quantum Key

<Steps>

1. Open the **KeyPair Management** window and click **New Keypair**. The
   **Generate Key** window opens.
2. On the **Identity** step, fill in your name (and email, if you want), then
   click **Continue**.
3. On the **Key Type** step, choose a post-quantum option:
   - The **Hybrid encryption** choice: messages to you are protected by ML-KEM
     as well as a classic algorithm. Signatures still use Ed25519.
   - Or open **Advanced options**, then **Other profile**, and pick a profile
     under the **Post-Quantum** heading. The **Full PQC Hybrid** profiles also
     use ML-DSA for signing.
   - Or turn on **Expert Mode**. The step is then called **Key Details**. Pick
     an **Algorithm** under the **Post-Quantum** heading. For encryption,
     choose **ML-KEM** for the subkey. For signing, choose **ML-DSA** or an
     **SLH-DSA** option for the primary key.

   If you do not see a choice, the active engine cannot make it. Most of these
   options need the **rPGP** engine.
4. Click **Continue**. On the **Validity & Protection** step, choose how long
   the key is **Valid for**, then click **Continue** again. (In **Expert
   Mode**, you set **Expires** for each key on the **Key Details** step
   instead.)
5. On the **Review** step, check the details and click **Generate**.

</Steps>

In **Expert Mode**, the **Key format** choice is locked to v6 while a
post-quantum algorithm is selected.

That's it. You now hold a post-quantum OpenPGP key.

## Encrypt to Post-Quantum and Classic Keys Together

You may pick some recipients with post-quantum keys and some with classic keys
in the same message. GpgFrontend then shows a warning called **Mixed Recipient
Types**.

Here is why. The message is locked once, and every recipient gets a copy of
the same lock. If someone can break a classic recipient's key, they can read
the whole message. So the post-quantum protection does not hold for that
message.

- Click **Cancel** to go back. Then encrypt one message for the post-quantum
  recipients and a separate one for the others.
- Click **Encrypt Anyway** if that risk is fine for you.

<Aside type="note" title="New in v2.2.3">

The **Mixed Recipient Types** warning is new.

</Aside>

<Aside type="caution" title="Experimental, by design">

Post-quantum OpenPGP now has a finished standard, but support for it is still
**new**, both here and in other apps.

- These keys **don't work with classic PGP tools yet**.
- Treat them as a way to **try out and prepare for** the quantum-safe future.
  Do not rely on them alone to protect critical secrets today.

For long-term signing and identity that must work everywhere, keep using a classic
key (such as Ed25519) alongside your post-quantum experiments.

</Aside>

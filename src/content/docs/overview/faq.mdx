## Getting Started

<details>
<summary>How do I get GpgFrontend?</summary>

Go to the [Downloads](/overview/downloads/) page and click the download
button. Then follow [Getting Started](/overview/getting-started/) for the
install steps on your system.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Which operating systems does GpgFrontend support?</summary>

GpgFrontend runs on Windows, macOS, and Linux. On BSD you have to build it
from source. For the exact versions, see
[System Requirements](/appendix/system-requirement/).

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do I update GpgFrontend to the latest version?</summary>

Get the newest version from the [Downloads](/overview/downloads/) page. Then:

- **Windows**: run the new installer.
- **macOS**: replace the old app with the new one.
- **Linux**: replace the old AppImage file with the new one.

Your settings and keys are kept, unless the release notes say otherwise. If
you installed from an app store or a package manager, update there instead.

The built-in update check only offers newer releases from your own series
(for example, 2.2.x). Moving to a new series is a step you take yourself. See
the [Version Numbering Policy](/appendix/version-policy/).

</details>

<details>
<summary>How does GpgFrontend tell me about a new version?</summary>

Only if you allow it. The update check is off unless you turn it on. You can
do that on the last page of the **Welcome to GpgFrontend** window, which opens
at the first start, or in **Settings** > **Updates**.

When it is on, GpgFrontend checks quietly in the background when it starts, at
most once a day. It never pops up a window. If a newer version exists, a small
badge appears on **Help** > **Check for Updates**. Click it to see what is new
and to download it.

The check only asks GitHub Releases. See
[Finding Your Settings](/guides/settings/#the-updates-page).

The Flatpak and Mac App Store builds have no update check. Flathub and the App
Store deliver their updates.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Is there still a Qt5 build?</summary>

Not for v2.2.x. Qt is the toolkit GpgFrontend is built with, and v2.2.x needs
Qt6. Qt5 builds were only made for the older v2.1.x line. If your system
cannot run Qt6, for example Windows 7, you need a v2.1.x release. See
[Older Versions and Backup Sites](/overview/downloads/#older-versions-and-backup-sites)
and [System Requirements](/appendix/system-requirement/).

</details>

## OpenPGP and the Engines

<details>
<summary>How do OpenPGP, PGP, and GnuPG (GPG) relate?</summary>

- **OpenPGP** is the open standard for encrypted email, files, and digital
  signatures. The current version of the standard is
  [RFC 9580][rfc9580]. It replaced the older [RFC 4880][rfc4880].
- **PGP** was the original closed-source software from the 1990s. It is rarely
  used today, but people often say "PGP" when they mean the OpenPGP standard.
- **GnuPG (GPG)** is the most widely used free, open-source program that
  follows the OpenPGP standard.

Today, when people say "PGP encryption", they almost always mean the OpenPGP
standard. The tool they actually use is GnuPG (GPG) or another compatible
app.

GpgFrontend is an easy-to-use app for OpenPGP. It uses GnuPG as its main
engine. Since the v2.2.x series, it also offers a second engine, rPGP.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Which engines does GpgFrontend use?</summary>

The engine is the part of GpgFrontend that does the encryption work.
GpgFrontend has two:

- **GnuPG** is the default and main engine in the regular desktop edition. It
  is proven and widely used. It is the best choice if you need full
  compatibility with an existing GnuPG setup.
- **rPGP** is a second engine, written in Rust and built into GpgFrontend. It
  supports newer OpenPGP features, including version 6 of the standard
  (RFC 9580).

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the rPGP engine?</summary>

rPGP is the second engine, next to GnuPG. It is built into GpgFrontend, so
GpgFrontend can do everyday OpenPGP tasks even when GnuPG is not installed.
See the question above for how the two engines compare.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Does rPGP replace GnuPG in GpgFrontend?</summary>

No. In the regular GpgFrontend desktop edition, GnuPG is still the default and
main engine.

The rPGP engine is an extra. It is used where GnuPG is not available, such as
in the Mac App Store edition, and for newer OpenPGP features. Some advanced
GnuPG features, such as smart cards, do not work with rPGP.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Can I use the same keys with GnuPG and rPGP?</summary>

Not always. In GpgFrontend, GnuPG and rPGP store their keys separately. Some
key types, data formats, and newer OpenPGP features do not work the same way
in both engines.

If you need full compatibility with an existing GnuPG setup, use the regular
GpgFrontend desktop edition with the GnuPG engine.

</details>

## GpgFrontend Lite (Mac App Store)

<details>
<summary>Is GpgFrontend available on the Mac App Store?</summary>

Yes, as **GpgFrontend Lite**. It is a lightweight edition that uses the rPGP
engine and does not include GnuPG.

The regular GpgFrontend desktop edition is not on the Mac App Store. The next
question explains why. If you need the regular edition, get it from the
[Downloads](/overview/downloads/) page or with Homebrew.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why is there a separate GpgFrontend Lite edition?</summary>

Every Mac App Store app must run in the macOS App Sandbox. A sandbox is a
closed area that keeps an app away from the rest of your system.

The regular GpgFrontend desktop edition is built around GnuPG. It offers
advanced settings, works with a GnuPG setup you already have, and reaches
your normal desktop environment. These things do not fit inside the sandbox.

So GpgFrontend Lite is a separate edition, not a replacement for the regular
one. It uses the rPGP engine and keeps its keys inside the sandbox storage
that macOS manages for the app. It still handles the everyday OpenPGP tasks:
encryption, decryption, signing, verification, and local key management.

| Feature               | Regular Desktop Edition      | Lite Edition (App Store)          |
| :-------------------- | :--------------------------- | :-------------------------------- |
| **Engine**            | **GnuPG** (Primary) + rPGP   | **rPGP Only** (Pure Rust)         |
| **Environment**       | Full System Access           | macOS Sandbox (Enhanced Security) |
| **GnuPG Integration** | Yes (System GPG)             | No (Self-contained)               |
| **Best For**          | Professional & Critical Work | Modern v6 Workflows & Quick Tasks |

<Aside type="note" title="Technical background">

The sandbox-related challenges around bundling and integrating GnuPG in a
movable macOS App Bundle have been discussed in the GnuPG development community.

See also: [GnuPG and macOS App Sandbox][gnupg-sandbox-mail]

</Aside>

</details>

<details>
<summary>Where are keys stored in GpgFrontend Lite?</summary>

GpgFrontend Lite stores its keys on your Mac, inside the sandbox storage that
macOS manages for the app. It does not upload your keys or messages to any
server.

Removing the app or resetting your system can erase this storage. So back up
the OpenPGP keys you create or import in GpgFrontend Lite. If your keys are
lost, your encrypted data cannot be recovered.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Can GpgFrontend Lite use my existing GnuPG keyring?</summary>

No. GpgFrontend Lite does not include GnuPG and does not connect to it. So
it cannot directly use the keys you already have in GnuPG.

To use a key from another OpenPGP app, export the key from that app and
import it into GpgFrontend Lite. Be careful when you export secret keys, and
keep your backups safe.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Which version should I use: GpgFrontend or GpgFrontend Lite?</summary>

Use the regular GpgFrontend desktop edition if you need GnuPG, smart cards,
advanced settings, or full compatibility with an existing GnuPG setup.

Use GpgFrontend Lite if you prefer a simple Mac App Store app for everyday
OpenPGP tasks on macOS.

</details>

## Why GPG / OpenPGP

<details>
<summary>Why choose GPG/OpenPGP over other encryption tools?</summary>

- **Open source and reviewed**: GPG is open-source, free, and maintained by a
  large community. Its code has been reviewed for decades, which builds
  security and trust.
- **You are not tied to one company**: it is fully based on an open standard.
  It works across major platforms, email apps, and tools. You can take your
  keys and data anywhere.
- **Full set of features**: encryption with a passphrase or with keys, digital
  signatures, key management, key revocation, and the "web of trust" (a way
  for users to confirm each other's keys).
- **Transparency and control**: every encryption step is visible and
  adjustable. You decide how your data is secured, with no hidden algorithms
  or automation.
- **Works together**: it is compatible with any software that follows OpenPGP,
  so your data stays easy to read and share.

Closed-source tools may limit which platforms they run on, hide how data is
encrypted, or tie you to their own products.

<Aside>
  GPG/OpenPGP lets you choose your own balance of privacy and convenience. It's
  like a "manual transmission" for digital security: maximum control, no forced
  automation, and nothing is hidden unless you want it to be.
</Aside>

</details>

<details>
<summary>How secure is GPG?</summary>

When used correctly, GPG gives strong protection:

- Strong algorithms and key sizes are available, in line with modern standards.
- Open source: the code is constantly reviewed by experts around the world.
- No mass leaks: no major flaw has ever led to a wide theft of GPG private
  keys.
- Most risks come from user mistakes, not from GPG itself. Examples are weak
  passphrases, badly stored keys, and malware on the device.

In short: how you look after your keys and passphrase matters just as much as
the software you use.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Has GnuPG ever been successfully attacked?</summary>

According to the official [GnuPG FAQ
11.8][gnupg-faq]:

> "If you mean, 'has GnuPG traffic ever been successfully cryptanalyzed?', the
> answer is a flat 'no'. We are unaware of any credible reports of any of the
> ciphers used in GnuPG having ever been successfully cryptanalyzed."

"Cryptanalyzed" means that someone broke the encryption itself.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What makes GPG/OpenPGP different from instant messengers or built-in encryption?</summary>

- No accounts or servers: you don't need to register or rely on any service.
- Long-term trust: keys can be used for years, to sign files, email, and
  software, and to build a "web of trust".
- Works offline: encrypt or sign files and send them any way you like, with no
  internet needed.
- Manual and open: everything is under your control, with no forced automation
  or cloud syncing.

Instant messengers (Signal, Matrix, etc.) are great for real-time chat. But
they need accounts, central servers, and frequent key changes. So they are
less suited to offline or anonymous use.

</details>

<details>
<summary>When is GPG/OpenPGP the best tool?</summary>

- Secure file exchange with strangers: journalists, whistleblowers, or anyone
  who needs to pass on documents anonymously.
- Publishing signed content: software releases, scientific results, or public
  statements that people must be able to check for years.
- Air-gapped environments: moving encrypted data between computers that are
  never connected to the internet.
- Full transparency: every encryption step is visible and can be checked.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why not just use password-based encryption?</summary>

Public-key encryption (as in GPG) uses a pair of keys: a public key that you
share, and a private key that you keep. It is more flexible and secure:

- No need to share passwords in advance; you only publish your public key.
- Anyone can encrypt for you, even if you've never met.
- Digital signatures prove who wrote a message and that it wasn't changed.
- Keys can be revoked or replaced at any time without breaking old data.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Can I use GPG with keys or data from other programs?</summary>

Yes. If your keys and encrypted data are in an OpenPGP-compatible format,
GnuPG and GpgFrontend can work with them. This includes most keys and data
from other GPG programs and from older PGP programs.

</details>

## Using GpgFrontend

<details>
<summary>What if I see "GnuPG not installed correctly"?</summary>

This message mainly applies to GpgFrontend versions before **2.2.0**, or to
tasks that need GnuPG in the regular desktop edition.

Since GpgFrontend **2.2.0**, if GnuPG is not available, GpgFrontend switches
to the built-in rPGP engine. The app still starts and offers the basic OpenPGP
features.

To use the GnuPG engine, the keys you already have in GnuPG, smart cards, or
other GnuPG-only features, install GnuPG:

- **macOS:** Install GnuPG using Homebrew with `brew install gnupg`.
- **Linux:** Install GnuPG via your package manager, for example
  `sudo apt install gpg` or `sudo dnf install gnupg`.
- **Windows:** Recent regular desktop builds include GnuPG. If needed, you can
  also download GnuPG from the [official GnuPG website][gnupg-download].

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do I import or export keys in GpgFrontend?</summary>

1. In the main window, click **Keys** > **Manage Keys**. The **KeyPair
   Management** window opens.
2. Open its **Key** menu. It has **Import Key** and **Export Key**.

You can also drag key files and drop them onto the **Key ToolBox**, the key
list in the main window. See
[Import and Export Keys](/guides/import-export-key-pair/).

</details>

<details>
<summary>Can I encrypt and decrypt files as well as text?</summary>

Yes, GpgFrontend handles both text and files. See
[File Operations](/guides/file-operations/) and
[Text Operations](/guides/text-operations/).

</details>

<details>
<summary>Does GpgFrontend support OpenPGP smart cards or tokens?</summary>

Yes, if your GnuPG supports smart cards. Smart cards need the **GnuPG
engine**. The rPGP engine does not support smart cards or tokens.

To manage a card, click **Advanced** > **Open Smart Card Controller**. This
opens the **Smart Card Controller** window. Sandboxed builds (Flatpak and the
Mac App Store edition) do not have this window. See
[OpenPGP Smart Cards](/guides/smart-card/).

</details>

<details>
<summary>Is GpgFrontend available in my language?</summary>

GpgFrontend comes in these languages:

- English
- Chinese (Simplified)
- Chinese (Traditional)
- French
- German
- Italian
- Spanish
- Russian
- Polish
- Turkish

To change the language, open **Settings**, choose **General**, and pick your
language under **Language**. Want to help translate? See the
[translation guide](/appendix/translate-interface/).

</details>

<details>
<summary>I cannot find a setting. Where did the tabs go?</summary>

The settings window was rebuilt in v2.2.2. The row of tabs across the top is
gone. In its place there is a search box and a list of pages down the left
side.

Type what you are looking for into the search box. It matches page names and
the settings on them, so you do not have to guess. See [Finding Your
Settings](/guides/settings/).

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is a profile?</summary>

A profile is one complete GpgFrontend setup: its own keys, its own settings,
and its own saved state. You already have one, and one is enough for most
people.

Make a second one if you want to keep work and personal keys apart, or if you
want a setup you can carry to another computer. See
[Profiles](/advanced/profiles/).

</details>

<details>
<summary>Can I move my whole setup to another computer?</summary>

Yes. Open the **Profiles** menu and choose **Export This Profile**. That writes
your keys, settings and saved state into a single `.gfp` file, protected by a
passphrase you choose.

On the other computer, use **Import Profile File**, or just double-click the
file. See [Profiles](/advanced/profiles/).

The Mac App Store Lite edition has no **Profiles** menu. There, back up your
keys with a [KeyPackage](/advanced/key-package/) instead.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is IM Encrypt?</summary>

Normal encrypted text is a big block that chat apps often break. **IM Encrypt**
turns your message into one short line that pastes cleanly into any messenger.

You find it in the **Operations** menu, while a text tab is open. There are no
toolbar buttons for it.

The other person pastes the line into GpgFrontend and clicks **Decrypt** as
usual. See [Instant Messaging](/guides/instant-messaging/).

</details>

<details>
<summary>Where did my favourite keys go?</summary>

The **Favourite** tab was replaced by key categories in v2.2.2. Categories do
the same job and more: you can have as many as you like, name them, and give
each one a colour.

Old favourites are not carried over, so you need to make a category and put
those keys back in it. See [Key Categories](/guides/key-categories/).

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why can I no longer generate a DSA key?</summary>

DSA is an older key type. With the rPGP engine, you cannot make new DSA keys,
or new RSA keys below 3072 bits. The current OpenPGP standard (RFC 9580) says
new keys of these kinds must not be made.

Existing keys of these types still import and still work. The GnuPG engine
still offers both if you really need one.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What happened to the startup self-check?</summary>

It was removed in v2.2.2. GpgFrontend no longer verifies its own libraries
while starting.

It is still a good idea to check that your download is genuine. The best time
to do that is before you install. See [Code & Binary
Verify](/appendix/code-binary-verify/).

</details>

<details>
<summary>What are Nightly versions?</summary>

Nightly versions are test builds. They are made automatically from the
newest code, with new features and fixes that are still being tested. They
are less stable than official releases and may change in ways that break
things. Do not use them for important work. Get them from the
[Downloads page](/overview/downloads/#test-builds).

Nightly files are not signed with the maintainer's OpenPGP key. Instead, each
nightly file comes with a `.minisig` signature file. Always use it to check
your download. You can also check that GitHub built the files for this
project. See
[Check a Test Build](/appendix/code-binary-verify/#check-a-test-build).

Nightly downloads are larger than official ones, because they include
information for finding bugs. The app runs just as fast. Please report
problems and feedback on GitHub.

For how stable, beta and nightly builds relate, see [Stable, Beta, and
Nightly](/appendix/version-policy/#stable-beta-and-nightly) in the Version
Numbering Policy.

</details>

## Your Keys, Privacy, and Security

<details>
<summary>How does GpgFrontend handle passphrases or PINs?</summary>

This depends on the engine used by the active key database.

With the **GnuPG engine**, GpgFrontend never reads or stores your GPG key
passphrases or smart card PINs. GnuPG asks for them itself, with its own
helper programs (pinentry and `gpg-agent`), separate from GpgFrontend.

With the **rPGP engine**, there are no such helper programs. So GpgFrontend
asks for the passphrase itself and passes it to the rPGP engine. The engine
keeps it in memory for a while, so you do not have to type it again for every
task. It is never written to disk. You can set how long it is kept on the
**rPGP** page in **Settings**.

For GpgFrontend's own encryption (for example a KeyPackage), passphrases and
PINs are used only in memory and cleared right away. On the **Advanced** page
in **Settings**, you can change **Secure Level** and **Application Key
Protection**. See [Memory Security](/advanced/memory-security/) and
[App Secure Key](/advanced/app-secure-key/).

</details>

<details>
<summary>Can lost passwords or keys be recovered?</summary>

No. Neither GpgFrontend nor the maintainer can recover a lost passphrase or
private key. That is what keeps your data safe from everyone else. So back up
your private key and remember your passphrase.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How does GpgFrontend keep communication secure?</summary>

It uses a proven OpenPGP engine (GnuPG by default, or the rPGP engine when
chosen) with strong encryption algorithms. Only the intended recipients can
decrypt your messages.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What data does GpgFrontend collect?</summary>

GpgFrontend does not collect or send any personal or usage data. All encryption
runs locally. Update checks are off unless you turn them on, and there is no
tracking or analytics built in.

There is also no AI feature in the app, and nothing you do is ever sent to an
AI service. See the [AI Policy](/appendix/ai-policy/).

</details>

## Community and Support

<details>
<summary>Is GpgFrontend really free? Can I donate?</summary>

Yes, it is completely free, and no, the project does not take donations. There
is no paid version and no locked features. If someone asks you to pay for
GpgFrontend, it is not from this project. See
[Free & No Donations](/appendix/free-no-donation/) for the full story, and for
ways to help that matter more than money.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Is AI used to build GpgFrontend?</summary>

AI coding tools help write ordinary code, tests and documentation, under the
direction and review of the maintainer. There is no AI in the app itself, and
AI does not write the cryptography; that is done by GnuPG and rPGP. The
[AI Policy](/appendix/ai-policy/) explains the whole arrangement.

</details>
